A particularity in processing the data with age-weighted variables is that we need to use (or compute) the total carrying capacity demand (carrying_capacity_demand) and use it to calculate pressure instead of using totalIndividuals directly (i.e., pressure = carrying_capacity_demand / carrying_capacity). Although this variable can be exported through NetLogo’s Behavior Space, it was not specified for all massive Sobol sampled experiments, so it is necessary to calculate it using womenAgeStructure and menAgeStructure (see “library/density/compute_carrying_capacity_demand.R”).

The overall distribution of end states in v1.2.1 remains very similar to that observed in v1.2. Approximately 62.5% of simulations avoid extinction, compared with 62.3% in v1.2, indicating that weighting ecological pressure by age-specific demand has little effect on the overall probability of demographic persistence across the parameter space explored.

The more noticeable difference concerns the distribution of surviving states. Only 1,080 simulations, approximately 2.3% of all runs and 3.6% of surviving runs, end in the Overshooting category, compared with 1,724 in v1.2. Because younger and older individuals can contribute less than one adult-equivalent unit to carrying-capacity demand, a given population headcount can exert less pressure than under the unweighted formulation. Demand weighting therefore reduces the frequency with which populations exceed the nominal capacity in demand terms without substantially altering their probability of survival.

Residence rule and Coale-Demeny region again have only minor aggregate effects. Patrilocal-patrilineal residence produces marginally more surviving simulations than matrilocal-matrilineal residence (15,031 versus 14,962), while overshooting is almost equally rare under both rules (536 versus 544 cases, respectively). These differences are too small to support a general residence-specific ranking of demographic resilience.

The sampled trajectories retain the regulated behaviour observed in v1.2. After an initial phase of demographic expansion, both residence systems converge towards comparatively stable population levels rather than continuing unrestricted growth. The introduction of age-weighted demand therefore preserves the basic negative density-dependent dynamics of the preceding version.

The principal difference is that population abundance and ecological pressure are now partially decoupled. Population headcounts can numerically exceed the carrying_capacity parameter without implying equivalent overshoot, because the density effect is determined by age-weighted carrying-capacity demand rather than by the number of individuals alone. Populations with similar headcounts may consequently experience different mortality pressure depending on their age composition.

In this targeted configuration, matrilocal-matrilineal populations tend to maintain somewhat larger headcounts than patrilocal-patrilineal populations, but this should be interpreted as a conditional trajectory difference rather than as evidence of a general residence-rule effect.

Under this particular parameter configuration, all sampled trajectories are classified as Overshooting according to the operational regulation criteria. As in the corresponding v1.2 experiment, however, this classification does not describe unstable runaway growth. Late-run pressures remain tightly concentrated near unity and show very low coefficients of variation, indicating stable regulation close to the nominal demand capacity.

Demand weighting changes the interpretation of this regulated state. Pressure now measures total age-weighted carrying-capacity demand relative to environmental capacity rather than headcount relative to capacity. Consequently, a population may contain substantially more individuals than the numerical value of carrying_capacity while remaining regulated close to a pressure of one.

The two residence systems again differ only modestly in their late-run mean pressure. The targeted sample therefore reinforces the broader result that demand weighting changes the demographic meaning of ecological load more than the qualitative form of density regulation itself.

The broad age-structure patterns remain almost unchanged from v1.2. Residence rule has a very small aggregate effect on median age ((d=0.076)), and regional differences are likewise modest. By contrast, the distinction between low- and high-pressure populations remains large under both matrilocal-matrilineal ((d=-1.13)) and patrilocal-patrilineal ((d=-1.26)) residence.

What changes in v1.2.1 is the causal architecture connecting age structure to ecological regulation. Pressure is now calculated from the age-weighted demand generated by the population, so demographic composition contributes directly to the mortality feedback rather than merely covarying with it. Nevertheless, the near-identity of the observed age-structure distributions with v1.2 shows that this added feedback does not substantially reorganise demographic composition over the explored parameter space. Fertility-driven differences in recruitment remain the dominant source of variation.

6.5.5 Interpretation

Across response variables, the v1.2.1 results preserve the broad parameter hierarchy established in v1.2. Fertility peak intensity remains the dominant demographic control, ranking first for both extinction probability and median age and second for population pressure. density_effect_scaling_factor remains the principal ecological regulator, ranking first for pressure and second for survival.

Nuptiality and fertility-schedule parameters provide the main secondary controls, with their relative importance strongly conditioned by residence rule. Baseline mortality and household-organisation parameters contribute comparatively little to the three responses examined.

Most importantly, introducing age-weighted demand produces remarkably little change in these rankings or in the parameter-specific age-structure effects. The new mechanism therefore modifies the pathway through which demographic composition contributes to ecological pressure without substantially changing which demographic processes dominate system behaviour.

6.6 Discussion notes

Version 1.2.1 extends the density-dependent framework by replacing population headcount with age-weighted carrying-capacity demand as the quantity exposed to ecological regulation. This introduces a direct link between demographic composition and mortality pressure while leaving the underlying density-response function unchanged.

Despite this conceptual modification, simulations show substantial behavioural continuity with v1.2. Overall survival remains virtually unchanged, the parameter hierarchy for extinction and median age is highly stable, and most parameter-specific effects on age structure are nearly identical. Demand weighting therefore does not create a new demographic regime within the parameter ranges explored.

Its clearest effect is instead on the interpretation and distribution of population pressure. Because individuals contribute different adult-equivalent demands according to age, population abundance is no longer interchangeable with ecological load. A population can contain more individuals than the numerical value of carrying_capacity without exerting equivalent pressure, while populations of similar size can differ in pressure because of their composition. Correspondingly, overshooting end states become less frequent than in v1.2.

Fertility peak intensity remains the central demographic driver. Higher fertility reduces extinction risk, increases ecological pressure, and produces younger age structures. density_effect_scaling_factor provides the principal countervailing ecological control, lowering pressure while increasing extinction risk among marginal demographic configurations. These opposing relationships remain essentially unchanged from v1.2.

Age structure now participates directly in ecological feedback, but the observed differences among age structures continue to arise primarily from reproductive recruitment. Low-pressure populations are older and contain fewer juveniles, while high-pressure populations are younger and more rapidly renewing. The similarity of these patterns to v1.2 indicates that the mechanical addition of age-weighted demand reinforces rather than reorganises the existing relationship between fertility, demographic turnover, and pressure.

Residence organisation again acts mainly as a modifier of sex-specific nuptiality. Female nuptiality parameters are relatively more influential under matrilocal-matrilineal residence, whereas male parameters are emphasised under patrilocal-patrilineal residence. Aggregate survival, household scaling, and median age remain only weakly differentiated by residence rule.

Version 1.2.1 should therefore be understood as a refinement of the ecological accounting in v1.2 rather than a qualitatively new regulatory regime. Its main contribution is to make ecological pressure composition-sensitive, preparing the model for v1.2.2, where demographic composition also determines the labour available to realise carrying capacity.

6.7 Chapter conclusion

Version 1.2.1 demonstrates that replacing population headcount with age-weighted subsistence demand changes the meaning of ecological pressure more than the overall demographic behaviour of the model. Survival frequency, age structure, and the principal sensitivity hierarchy remain close to v1.2, while overshooting becomes less frequent and population size is partially decoupled from ecological load. Fertility peak intensity continues to generate demographic demand, and density-effect strength continues to regulate it, but pressure now also reflects who composes the population rather than simply how many individuals are present. This composition-sensitive demand provides the necessary foundation for v1.2.2, where age structure also determines productive labour and therefore the effective supply side of carrying capacity.
