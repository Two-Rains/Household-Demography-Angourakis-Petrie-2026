8.2 Experiment design

Sobol sampling is used to generate a diverse set of parameter combinations across the defined parameter space. This allows us to explore how different parameter configurations affect the model’s outcomes and to identify which parameters are most influential in driving population survival or extinction. The actual sampling was done with library/generate_sobol.R script, which generates a sample with 2,000 parameter combinations across the parameter ranges defined in parameter_ranges.R. Note that given the higher computational cost of daily iterations, a nested subset of 512 combinations were subsequently selected to generate sufficiently-robust simulation results that can be compared to the yearly versions.

The 512-subset of the generated parameter combinations were then used to run simulations in NetLogo’s Behaviour Space, for each combination of residence-rule (“matrilineal-matrilocal” and “patrilineal-patrilocal”) and coale-demeny-region (“north”, “south”, “east”, and “west”), varying the SEED parameter (n = 3) to introduce stochasticity, for a total of 12,288 simulations. Moreover, all runs assume a fixed maturity-age-in-years of 15 years, a year length of 365 days (no leap years), and a gestation period of 280 days (40 weeks). Remember that maturity-age-in-years determines a conservative age solely for social maturity, used as a criterion for household viability, while union-formation and reproductive maturity are regulated probabilistically through the Peristera-Kostaki parameters in the fertility and nuptiality submodels.

As with v1.1, the variable survival is created during preprocessing to classify whether populations get extinct, survive and/or grow within the maximum number of simulation steps (max-iterations).

Because of the sheer number of daily iterations required to approach comparable time scales of previous versions, trajectories datasets are simply too large to be loaded conventionally in R. For this reason, we need to use the function argument ignored_variables (alternatively also selected_variables is available) to skip loading unnecessary variables, as well as potentially restricting the span of the simulation data to a subset of iteration steps (start_step, end_step). The different trajectory-type data frames must be loaded, preprocessed, saved as RData, and clear, so that only one is in memory simultaneously.

Age structures of surviving populations are prepared by categorising them according to the same grouping variables used in the sensitivity analysis, including categorical parameters (i.e., residence_rule, coale_demeny_region) and parameters converted into binary categories based on their median value, exclusively those regulating demographic probabilities (e.g., mortality_regime, fertility_intensity, etc.).

Furthermore, this selection includes the categorical variable survival that distinguishes between populations that became extinct, persisted without reaching the maximum population size, and reached the maximum population size. This allows us to compare the age structures of surviving populations across different demographic regimes and residence systems, and to identify patterns or differences associated with population persistence or growth.

Moreover, a set of indicators is computed for each age structure, including median age, dependency ratios, and measures of age-structure irregularity, which can provide further insights into the demographic characteristics of surviving populations and how they relate to different parameter regimes and residence systems. These indicators can help identify specific features of the age structure associated with population persistence or growth, and understand how different demographic regimes and residence systems may influence the age structure of surviving populations. Namely, the indicators computed include:

  • median_age: the age that divides the population into two halves, with 50% of the population being younger and 50% being older.
  • youth_share: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of dependents in the population.
  • reproductive_share: the ratio of the population aged 15-64 to the population aged 65 and over, which indicates the proportion of reproductive-age individuals in the population.
  • elderly_share: the ratio of the population aged 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of elderly dependents in the population.
  • total_dependency_ratio: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 and 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the overall proportion of dependent individuals in the population.

The transition to daily iteration shifts the distribution of demographic outcomes relative to v1.1, but extinction does not dominate the explored parameter space as strongly as previously inferred. Approximately half of the simulations survive to the end state, with a modestly higher survival frequency under matrilocal-matrilineal residence. Among surviving populations, Growth remains the most common outcome under both residence rules, accounting for roughly three quarters of survivors, while Persistence represents a substantially larger share than in v1.1.

Daily demographic scheduling therefore appears to reduce the frequency and tempo of unconstrained expansion without eliminating it. Differences associated with coale_demeny_region remain small, and residence-rule contrasts are modest relative to the overall effects of the demographic parameters explored.

8.8 Discussion notes

8.8.1 Same demographic foundations, altered quantitative hierarchy

Version 1.3 preserves the density-independent demographic architecture of v1.1 while replacing annual event scheduling with daily demographic processes and explicit gestation and postpartum amenorrhoea. Fertility peak intensity remains the dominant predictor of survival, final population size, and median age, demonstrating strong continuity in the model’s underlying demographic logic.

Mortality remains an important secondary predictor of survival and final size, but it no longer approaches fertility peak intensity in importance and has little direct effect on median age. The daily model therefore preserves the main causal architecture of v1.1 while changing its quantitative expression rather than simply reproducing the same parameter hierarchy.

8.8.2 Growth becomes slower and persistence more common

Daily reproductive scheduling reduces demographic tempo. Growth remains the most common outcome among surviving populations, but Persistence occupies a noticeably larger share than in v1.1, and overall extinction is more frequent. Explicit gestation and postpartum amenorrhoea delay the conversion of reproductive opportunity into births, so viable populations take longer to accumulate demographic gains.

The targeted mortality and fertility sweeps reinforce this interpretation. Neither displays a sharp viability threshold under favourable conditions; instead, both parameters primarily alter the time required to approach the technical population ceiling.

8.8.3 Age structure becomes older, but Growth-Persistence separation does not strengthen

Surviving populations are systematically older in v1.3 than in v1.1. Growth populations have a mean median age of about 21.5 years and persistent populations about 30.8 years, compared with approximately 18.6 and 29.6 years in the annual baseline. The Persistence-Growth contrast nevertheless becomes slightly weaker in standardised terms ((d=1.73) versus (1.92)), not stronger.

Daily scheduling therefore shifts demographic composition towards older ages while preserving the strong association between rapid recruitment and youthful age structure.

8.8.4 Residence-rule differences remain secondary

Aggregate residence-rule effects remain small. Matrilocal-matrilineal populations are marginally older on average and show a slightly larger Persistence-Growth age contrast, while patrilocal-patrilineal populations grow somewhat faster in the targeted trajectory experiments. These differences are conditional and modest compared with the influence of fertility and reproductive scheduling.

The clearest residence effects continue to occur through sex-specific union parameters rather than through a general survival or age-structure advantage.

8.8.5 Explicit reproductive delays add information without replacing the baseline

Postpartum amenorrhoea provides the clearest genuinely new demographic control introduced by v1.3. Longer amenorrhoea modestly increases median age by extending birth intervals, but its influence remains secondary to fertility peak intensity and fertility spread.

The daily formulation therefore demonstrates that temporal resolution is a substantive modelling choice. It changes reproductive tempo, cohort structure, and the time required for population expansion while preserving the broad fertility-driven demographic logic of the annual baseline.

8.9 Chapter conclusion

Version 1.3 demonstrates that introducing daily demographic scheduling changes the quantitative expression of the Household Demography model without replacing its underlying demographic architecture. Fertility peak intensity remains the principal determinant of survival, population growth, and age composition, while explicit gestation and postpartum amenorrhoea slow reproductive turnover, increase the frequency of persistent outcomes, and produce generally older surviving populations.

Daily iteration therefore contributes more than finer numerical resolution: it makes reproductive delays and birth spacing explicit and changes the tempo through which demographic outcomes emerge. At the same time, the persistence of the baseline fertility-driven hierarchy provides a clear point of continuity with v1.1 and establishes the appropriate daily baseline for the density-regulated v1.4 series.
