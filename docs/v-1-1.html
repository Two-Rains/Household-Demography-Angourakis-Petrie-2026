4.2 Experiment design

Sobol sampling is used to generate a diverse set of parameter combinations across the defined parameter space. This allows us to explore how different parameter configurations affect the model’s outcomes and to identify which parameters are most influential in driving population survival or extinction. The actual sampling was done with generate_sobol.R script, which generates a sample with 2000 parameter combinations across the parameter ranges defined in parameter_ranges.R. The generated parameter combinations were then used to run simulations in NetLogo’s Behaviour Space, for each combination of residence-rule (“matrilineal-matrilocal” and “patrilineal-patrilocal”) and coale-demeny-region (“north”, “south”, “east”, and “west”), varying the SEED parameter (n = 3) to introduce stochasticity, for a total of 48,000 simulations. Moreover, all runs assume a fixed maturity-age of 15 years old. Note that this constant determines a conservative age solely for social maturity, used as a criterion for household viability, while union-formation and reproductive maturity are regulated probabilistically through the Peristera-Kostaki parameters in the fertility and nuptiality submodels.

Age structures of surviving populations are prepared by categorising them according to the same grouping variables used in the sensitivity analysis, including categorical parameters (i.e., residence_rule, coale_demeny_region) and parameters converted into binary categories based on their median value, exclusively those regulating demographic probabilites (e.g., mortality_regime, fertility_intensity, etc.).

Furthermore, this selection includes the categorical variable survival that distinguishes between populations that became extinct, persisted without reaching the maximum population size, and reached the maximum population size. This allows us to compare the age structures of surviving populations across different demographic regimes and residence systems, and to identify patterns or differences associated with population persistence or growth.

Moreover, a set of indicators is computed for each age structure, including median age, dependency ratios, and measures of age-structure irregularity, which can provide further insights into the demographic characteristics of surviving populations and how they relate to different parameter regimes and residence systems. These indicators can help identify specific features of the age structure associated with population persistence or growth, and understand how different demographic regimes and residence systems may influence the age structure of surviving populations. Namely, the indicators computed include:

  • median_age: the age that divides the population into two halves, with 50% of the population being younger and 50% being older.
  • youth_share: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of dependents in the population.
  • reproductive_share: the ratio of the population aged 15-64 to the population aged 65 and over, which indicates the proportion of reproductive-age individuals in the population.
  • elderly_share: the ratio of the population aged 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of elderly dependents in the population.
  • total_dependency_ratio: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 and 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the overall proportion of dependent individuals in the population.

The distinction between Persistence and Growth is associated with a very large difference in age structure. Persistent populations have a mean median age of 29.6 years, compared with 18.6 years among populations reaching max_population, and their juvenile representation is correspondingly much lower. Cohen’s (d=1.92) indicates a large separation between the two distributions.

Growth in this density-independent model is therefore associated with strong cohort renewal: rapidly expanding populations accumulate large juvenile cohorts and maintain comparatively young age structures. Populations that remain below the technical population ceiling are substantially older and less juvenile. The contrast should not be interpreted as two alternative demographic equilibria, since v1.1 contains no density-dependent stabilising mechanism; rather, it distinguishes configurations capable of rapid sustained growth from those producing much slower demographic turnover.

4.5.4.14 Interpretation

Results identify fertility peak intensity as the principal regulator of demographic youthfulness. c1_fert produces by far the largest differences in median age, while post-peak fertility spread (sigma2_fert) and several nuptiality parameters exert smaller secondary effects. Mortality intensity has only a modest effect on age structure among surviving populations.

The strongest structural contrast is instead between demographic outcomes. Populations that reach the growth threshold are much younger and contain substantially larger juvenile cohorts than populations that merely persist, yielding a very large Persistence-versus-Growth effect ((d=1.92)).

This reflects differences in cohort renewal rather than alternative equilibria: v1.1 has no density-dependent mechanism capable of stabilising population size.

Residence organisation has little direct effect on aggregate median age ((d=0.045)), although it modifies the sensitivity of age structure to particular male and female nuptiality parameters. Results therefore point to a hierarchy in which demographic regime and reproductive schedules dominate population structure, while residence rules act mainly as interaction modifiers.

4.8 Discussion notes

The v1.1 results identify fertility intensity as the dominant control on baseline demographic behaviour. c1_fert is by far the strongest predictor of extinction, surviving population size, and median age. Female nuptiality and fertility-schedule shape form an important secondary group of controls, whereas mortality intensity remains directionally important but is much less dominant than indicated by the previous simulation results.

Survival is therefore not governed by a single mortality threshold. Instead, extinction emerges from the combined capacity of the demographic system to replace individuals through fertility and union formation. Partial dependence shows a particularly strong nonlinear response to fertility intensity, while female union timing, union intensity, and fertility spread alter the probability of successful replacement more gradually.

Population composition exhibits an even clearer hierarchy. The largest age-structure difference is between persistent and rapidly growing populations, followed by variation in fertility intensity. Residence rule by itself produces almost no shift in aggregate median age, although it changes the importance of sex-specific nuptiality parameters and generates modest differences in household and trajectory dynamics.

Taken together, v1.1 behaves as a density-independent demographic growth system in which fertility and reproductive scheduling determine whether replacement is sufficient and how rapidly populations expand. Kinship organisation modifies these processes but does not override them.

4.8.1 Residence rule

Residence rule has a small aggregate effect on survival in the global exploration. Patrilocal-patrilineal runs in fact produce marginally more non-extinction outcomes than matrilocal-matrilineal runs, but the difference is too small to support a general ranking of demographic resilience.

Residence organisation matters more through interactions with other mechanisms. In targeted trajectories, sex-asymmetric nuptiality can generate differences in growth tempo, and the random-forest analyses show that female nuptiality parameters tend to become more influential under matrilocal-matrilineal residence whereas male parameters are emphasised under patrilocal-patrilineal residence. Residence rule therefore acts mainly as a modifier of union formation, migration, and household organisation rather than as an independent determinant of survival.

4.8.2 Other parameters

Parameters describing initial household configuration and kinship restrictions generally have much lower importance than fertility and nuptiality variables. Some retain detectable effects, particularly on surviving population size or median age, but none approaches the influence of fertility intensity.

coale_demeny_region also produces relatively small aggregate differences. Regional mortality schedules generate modest variation in end-state survival and age structure, but the present experiments do not isolate which age- or sex-specific components of the regional life tables are responsible. Any mechanistic interpretation of these regional contrasts would therefore require a more targeted decomposition of the Coale-Demeny schedules.

4.8.3 Variability of population structure

Results reveal selective rather than general structural robustness. Age structure is remarkably insensitive to residence rule and only modestly affected by Coale-Demeny region, but it changes strongly with demographic growth regime and fertility intensity. Populations reaching the growth threshold are much younger than persistent populations, and high-fertility regimes substantially increase juvenile representation.

This distinction is important when considering model simplification. Aggregate models that omit age and household structure may reproduce broad changes in total population size when social organisation is not central to the research question. They would, however, miss substantial changes in demographic composition associated with fertility and growth tempo, as well as the residence-dependent interaction of male and female nuptiality. The usefulness of a simpler formulation therefore depends on whether the target phenomenon concerns population abundance alone or the composition and organisation through which that abundance is generated.

4.9 Chapter conclusion

Version 1.1 establishes a density-independent baseline in which fertility intensity and reproductive scheduling dominate population survival, growth rate, and age composition, while residence organisation primarily modifies nuptiality, household dynamics, and demographic tempo. In the absence of ecological regulation, viable parameter combinations generally produce continued expansion towards the imposed population ceiling, providing the reference behaviour against which the density-dependent model versions can be evaluated.
