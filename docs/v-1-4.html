9.2 Experiment design

Sobol sampling is used to generate a diverse set of parameter combinations across the defined parameter space. This allows us to explore how different parameter configurations affect the model’s outcomes and to identify which parameters are most influential in driving population survival or extinction. The actual sampling was done with library/generate_sobol.R script, which generates a sample with 2,000 parameter combinations across the parameter ranges defined in parameter_ranges.R. Note that given the higher computational cost of daily iterations, a nested subset of 512 combinations were subsequently selected to generate sufficiently-robust simulation results that can be compared to the yearly versions.

Compared to v1.3, and as a parallel to v1.2, the sampled configurations for v1.4 include carrying_capacity and density_effect_scaling_factor.

As with v1.3, the 512-subset of the generated parameter combinations were then used to run simulations in NetLogo’s Behaviour Space, for each combination of residence-rule (“matrilineal-matrilocal” and “patrilineal-patrilocal”) and coale-demeny-region (“north”, “south”, “east”, and “west”), varying the SEED parameter (n = 3) to introduce stochasticity, for a total of 12,288 simulations. Moreover, all runs assume a fixed maturity-age-in-years of 15 years, a year length of 365 days (no leap years), and a gestation period of 280 days (40 weeks). Remember that maturity-age-in-years determines a conservative age solely for social maturity, used as a criterion for household viability, while union-formation and reproductive maturity are regulated probabilistically through the Peristera-Kostaki parameters in the fertility and nuptiality submodels.

As with all other versions, the variable survival is created during preprocessing to classify whether populations get extinct, survive with certain size, within the maximum number of simulation steps (max-iterations). As in v1.2.x, pressure (and final_pressure for trajectories) emerges as a measure of population pressure, calculated during preprocess_simdata(), as totalIndividuals / carrying_capacity.

Because of the sheer number of daily iterations required to approach comparable time scales of previous versions, trajectories datasets are simply too large to be loaded conventionally in R. For this reason, we need to use the function argument ignored_variables (alternatively also selected_variables is available) to skip loading unnecessary variables, as well as potentially restricting the span of the simulation data to a subset of iteration steps (start_step, end_step). The different trajectory-type data frames must be loaded, preprocessed, saved as RData, and cleared, so that only one is in memory simultaneously.

As in previous versions, age structures of surviving populations are prepared by categorising them according to the same grouping variables used in the sensitivity analysis, as well as the categorical variable survival, which now takes different categories in response to pressure.

Again, a set of indicators is computed that aims to help identify specific features of the age structure associated with parametric conditions. Namely:

  • median_age: the age that divides the population into two halves, with 50% of the population being younger and 50% being older.
  • youth_share: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of dependents in the population.
  • reproductive_share: the ratio of the population aged 15-64 to the population aged 65 and over, which indicates the proportion of reproductive-age individuals in the population.
  • elderly_share: the ratio of the population aged 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of elderly dependents in the population.
  • total_dependency_ratio: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 and 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the overall proportion of dependent individuals in the population.

Across the explored parameter space, 6,585 simulations avoid extinction, corresponding to approximately 53.6% of the daily-model experiment. Surviving populations are distributed predominantly between low- and high-pressure persistence, with low-pressure states forming the larger group. Overshooting occurs only rarely at the end state.

Population pressure is concentrated well below unity, with a broad mode around 0.3–0.4, showing that density-dependent mortality can maintain viable populations substantially below nominal carrying capacity. The Coale-Demeny regional mortality families generate only modest differences in the overall distribution of outcomes.

Residence rule introduces a small but consistent asymmetry. Matrilocal-matrilineal populations show a somewhat higher frequency of survival and a slightly larger share of high-pressure states than patrilocal-patrilineal populations. The difference remains modest relative to the effects of the continuously varied demographic and density-regulation parameters and should therefore be interpreted as a secondary interaction rather than an intrinsic resilience advantage.

Under the selected parameter configuration, both residence systems remain viable throughout the simulation horizon and settle into density-regulated population bands. Population sizes fluctuate substantially at the daily scale, but their long-run medians remain bounded rather than undergoing sustained expansion.

The residence systems nevertheless occupy different regulatory positions. Population pressure is strongly associated with demographic composition. Low-pressure populations are substantially older, with smaller juvenile cohorts and lower dependency ratios, whereas high-pressure populations are younger and more strongly dominated by recent demographic recruitment. The difference in median age between low- and high-pressure persistence and overshooting is large (Cohen’s (d=-0.94)), confirming that pressure regime captures a major structural distinction among surviving populations.

This relationship is consistent with the demographic mechanisms driving population pressure. Parameter combinations supporting more rapid cohort renewal simultaneously produce younger populations and sustain greater population abundance relative to carrying capacity, whereas slower renewal is associated with older populations maintained at lower pressure. Population pressure in v1.4 therefore reflects not only proximity to the environmental limit but also the reproductive tempo and age composition through which that population level is generated.

Overshooting populations display age structures broadly similar to high-pressure persistent populations, suggesting that the main demographic transition occurs between low- and high-pressure regimes rather than specifically at the carrying-capacity threshold.

9.5.4.15 Interpretation

Across the parameter-specific analyses, fertility remains the principal determinant of age structure in v1.4. Fertility peak intensity produces the largest effect by a wide margin, while fertility spread and several union-formation parameters provide secondary controls over cohort renewal.

Postpartum amenorrhoea contributes a smaller but consistent ageing effect through longer birth intervals. Baseline mortality, by contrast, has almost no direct effect on median age among surviving populations despite retaining importance for survival and population pressure.

Density-dependent regulation therefore differentiates the roles of the model’s mechanisms: fertility and union formation primarily structure the composition of surviving populations, while ecological feedback and mortality more strongly determine whether populations persist and where they stabilise relative to carrying capacity.

The targeted experiment isolates the regulatory effect of density_effect_scaling_factor particularly clearly. When the scaling factor is zero, density-dependent mortality is absent and populations grow rapidly towards the technical population ceiling, reproducing the behaviour of the density-independent daily baseline.

Introducing even a very small positive density effect transforms the dynamics from sustained expansion to stable regulation. At density_effect_scaling_factor = 0.01, populations stabilise above nominal carrying capacity; around 0.05 they remain close to capacity; and progressively larger values maintain increasingly smaller populations below it.

The relationship is therefore monotonic rather than threshold-like. Density-effect strength controls the equilibrium level of the population relative to environmental capacity, while all tested positive values remain viable under this favourable demographic configuration.

The two residence systems respond almost identically across the sweep. In this experiment, the regulatory strength of density-dependent mortality greatly exceeds any residence-specific difference in the realised equilibrium population.

9.7 Discussion notes

Version 1.4 combines daily demographic scheduling with head-count-based density-dependent mortality. The resulting system retains the fertility-driven demographic architecture of the daily baseline while introducing an ecological feedback that regulates population abundance relative to nominal carrying capacity.

Fertility peak intensity remains the strongest determinant of demographic viability and age composition. Density-effect strength becomes the principal ecological control, ranking second for survival and first for population pressure. Baseline mortality remains relevant to survival and pressure but contributes little to age structure among populations that persist.

The end-state exploration shows that surviving populations are most commonly maintained under low population pressure, although a substantial minority persist at high pressure and rare populations overshoot nominal capacity. The targeted scaling experiment demonstrates why these different equilibria arise: increasing density_effect_scaling_factor progressively lowers the population maintained relative to carrying capacity, while removing the density effect restores unrestricted growth.

Daily scheduling does not make regulation intrinsically unstable. In the reference trajectory experiment, late-run coefficients of variation are very low under both residence systems. Instead, residence organisation influences where populations sit within the high-pressure regime. Matrilocal-matrilineal trajectories more frequently cross the nominal capacity and are therefore classified as Overshooting, whereas patrilocal-patrilineal trajectories predominantly remain in the Stress-prone regime below capacity.

Age structure remains closely coupled to population pressure. Low-pressure populations are older and less juvenile, whereas high-pressure and overshooting populations are younger and demographically more rapidly renewing. This association arises because the same reproductive processes that replenish young cohorts also increase the demographic pressure placed on the regulated system.

Residence rule has only a small aggregate effect on survival and age structure, although it modifies several sex-specific union parameters and the location of trajectories relative to carrying capacity. Its effects should therefore be understood as interactional rather than as evidence that either residence system is generally more resilient.

Overall, v1.4 shows that daily demographic timing and ecological regulation act on different dimensions of system behaviour. Daily scheduling structures reproductive tempo and cohort formation, while density-dependent mortality determines the population level that those demographic processes can sustain.

9.8 Chapter conclusion

Version 1.4 establishes the daily head-count-based density-regulated formulation of the Household Demography model. Fertility peak intensity remains the dominant demographic control on survival and age structure, while density_effect_scaling_factor emerges as the principal determinant of population pressure and a major secondary determinant of viability.

Surviving populations occupy a range of regulated states, most commonly below the high-pressure threshold, while targeted experiments show that density-effect strength continuously controls equilibrium population size from unrestricted growth at zero regulation to increasingly low-pressure persistence under stronger feedback. Residence organisation modifies particular union-formation effects and the realised position of populations relative to carrying capacity, but remains secondary to fertility and density regulation at the aggregate level.

The model therefore provides the appropriate daily counterpart to v1.2 and the baseline from which the demand-weighted daily versions can be evaluated.
