5.2 Experiment design

Sobol sampling is used to generate a diverse set of parameter combinations across the defined parameter space. This allows us to explore how different parameter configurations affect the model’s outcomes and to identify which parameters are most influential in driving population survival or extinction. The actual sampling was done with generate_sobol.R script, which generates a sample with 2,000 parameter combinations across the parameter ranges defined in parameter_ranges.R. Compared to v1.1, the sampled configurations for v1.2 introduce two additional parameters: carrying_capacity and density_effect_scaling_factor. These were generated previously with library/generate_sobol.R.

As with v1.1, the generated parameter combinations were then used to run simulations in NetLogo’s Behaviour Space, for each combination of residence-rule (“matrilineal-matrilocal” and “patrilineal-patrilocal”) and coale-demeny-region (“north”, “south”, “east”, and “west”), varying the SEED parameter (n = 3) to introduce stochasticity, for a total of 48,000 simulations. Moreover, all runs assume a fixed maturity-age of 15 years old. Note that this constant determines a conservative age solely for social maturity, used as a criterion for household viability, while union-formation and reproductive maturity are regulated probabilistically through the Peristera-Kostaki parameters in the fertility and nuptiality submodels.

In this version, survival also exists to differentiate when populations get extinct or survive in qualitatively different states. However, the calculation and interpretation of survival categories are not the same as in v1.1. Before the introduction of the density effect on mortality, “growth” cases were identified as those where births exceeded deaths, producing unbounded exponential growth, which then necessitated imposing max_population to stop simulations. “Persistence” is then defined as all cases where populations are sustained below this limit for a relatively long time (max_iterations).

In v1.2, as in all other subsequent versions with this density component, surviving population trajectories will flatten around a value depending on the interaction of carrying_capacity and the other parameters regulating the density effect on mortality, which may or may not be at a value beyond the technical limit of simulations (i.e. compare max_population with the range explored for carrying_capacity).

Given this difference, and in the sake of retaining a relative version comparability, we now consider as “overshooting” all cases where end state populations are found above the corresponding carrying capacity, and distinguish between “persistence with low pressure” and “persistence with high pressure”, when end state persistent populations are respectively within the first and second half of the carrying capacity. In line with this, a novelty of v1.2 is the creation of pressure (and final_pressure for trajectories) as a measure of population pressure, calculated during preprocess_simdata(), as totalIndividuals / carrying_capacity.

The assessment of pressure allows us to interpret survival categories in terms of whether population persistence occurs under a regulated or high-pressure demographic regime. Unfortunately, any cases of overshooting where carrying capacities are set beyond max_population will be misclassified as “persistence”.

Additionally, as v1.2 qualitatively changes population dynamics, a few metrics are calculated to assess sampled trajectories. These are: peak_population, peak_pressure, equilibrium_pressure_mean and equilibrium_pressure_variance.

Besides collapse (extinction) and overshooting (pass a threshold beyond the carrying capacity), the regulation regimes are classified based on equilibrium_pressure_mean, equilibrium_pressure_variance and final_pressure:

As in v1.1, age structures of surviving populations are prepared by categorising them according to the same grouping variables used in the sensitivity analysis, as well as the categorical variable survival, which now takes different categories in response to pressure.

Again, a set of indicators is computed that aims to help identify specific features of the age structure associated with parametric conditions. Namely:

  • median_age: the age that divides the population into two halves, with 50% of the population being younger and 50% being older.
  • youth_share: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of dependents in the population.
  • reproductive_share: the ratio of the population aged 15-64 to the population aged 65 and over, which indicates the proportion of reproductive-age individuals in the population.
  • elderly_share: the ratio of the population aged 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the proportion of elderly dependents in the population.
  • total_dependency_ratio: the ratio of the population aged 0-14 and 65 and over to the population aged 15-64, which indicates the overall proportion of dependent individuals in the population.

In contrast to the previous interpretation, the introduction of density-dependent mortality does not substantially increase the overall probability of population survival. Population pressure is strongly associated with demographic composition. Low-pressure surviving populations are substantially older, with a mean median age of 22.6 years, lower juvenile representation, and lower dependency ratios. High-pressure populations are considerably younger, while overshooting populations display the youngest age structures and the largest relative juvenile cohorts.

The contrast between low- and high-pressure regimes is large (Cohen’s (d=-1.17)), indicating that pressure in v1.2 captures more than relative population abundance. These older low-pressure populations should not be characterised as demographically unstable on the basis of age structure alone. In the density-regulated model, an older structure can instead reflect a viable population in which recruitment is sufficient for persistence without generating the large juvenile cohorts associated with rapid expansion.

5.5.4.14 Interpretation

Across analyses, age structure is governed primarily by reproductive renewal. Fertility peak intensity is the strongest common predictor, while fertility spread and sex-specific nuptiality parameters provide important secondary controls. Which nuptiality parameters matter most depends on residence organisation, with female schedules receiving greater weight under matrilocal-matrilineal residence and male schedules under patrilocal-patrilineal residence.

Baseline mortality has a substantially weaker direct effect on age composition than previously inferred. Density-related parameters also contribute less to median age than to population pressure or extinction probability. Ecological regulation therefore shapes the demographic regime within which populations persist, whereas fertility and union formation determine much of the internal age structure of those surviving populations.

The strong relationship between pressure and age structure integrates these results: parameter combinations that generate rapid recruitment tend to produce younger populations, larger juvenile cohorts, and higher dependency ratios, which in turn are associated with higher population pressure.

5.7 Discussion notes

Version 1.2 introduces the first explicit negative density-dependent feedback into the Household Demography model. Its principal effect is not to increase the overall probability of survival relative to v1.1, which remains almost unchanged, but to transform the dynamics of surviving populations. Unrestricted expansion is replaced by regulated states distributed across a continuum of population pressure.

Fertility peak intensity remains the dominant demographic determinant. c1_fert is the strongest predictor of extinction and median age and the second strongest predictor of population pressure. High fertility simultaneously reduces extinction risk, increases population pressure, and produces younger age structures. These linked effects identify reproductive recruitment as the main demographic force pushing populations toward ecological constraint.

Density-effect strength provides the corresponding ecological control. density_effect_scaling_factor is the second strongest predictor of extinction and overwhelmingly the strongest predictor of population pressure. Increasing it lowers the population maintained relative to carrying capacity, but also modestly raises extinction probability across the broader parameter space. Density regulation therefore introduces a genuine demographic-ecological trade-off: stronger regulation reduces pressure while making otherwise marginal demographic configurations less viable.

In contrast, the nominal carrying_capacity parameter itself has relatively little predictive importance within the range explored. It sets the scale against which density load is measured, but the realised position of the population relative to that limit is determined more strongly by fertility, nuptiality, baseline mortality, and the intensity of the density response.

Age structure provides another expression of this coupling. Low-pressure populations are older and have lower juvenile dependency, whereas high-pressure and overshooting populations are increasingly dominated by younger cohorts. Population pressure therefore reflects demographic turnover as well as abundance: rapid recruitment generates both the population growth that increases pressure and the juvenile dependency associated with young expanding populations.

Residence organisation remains a secondary modifier. Aggregate survival and median age differ only slightly between residence systems, and household scaling is almost identical. Its clearest influence appears instead in the relative importance of sex-specific nuptiality parameters, with female schedules contributing more strongly under matrilocal-matrilineal residence and male schedules under patrilocal-patrilineal residence.

Overall, v1.2 converts the density-independent demographic engine of v1.1 into a regulated demographic-ecological system without replacing its demographic foundations. Fertility determines much of the pressure placed on the system, while density-dependent mortality determines how strongly that pressure is translated back into demographic constraint.

5.8 Chapter conclusion

Version 1.2 demonstrates that adding density-dependent mortality fundamentally changes population dynamics without substantially changing the overall frequency of demographic persistence. Viable populations no longer expand indefinitely but converge toward regulated states whose position relative to nominal carrying capacity is controlled primarily by fertility intensity and the strength of density-dependent mortality. c1_fert remains the central demographic driver, while density_effect_scaling_factor emerges as the principal ecological regulator; baseline mortality, carrying capacity, household parameters, and residence rule play more secondary or context-dependent roles. The resulting coupling between demographic recruitment, population pressure, and age structure provides the baseline density-regulated behaviour against which the demand- and labour-weighted versions can be evaluated.
